Some of the best work I've done had no ticket, no spec, and no obvious right
answer. At Spekit, a set of cross-platform features we revamped ended up
protecting over $1M in monthly revenue by cutting churn. None of it started with a
plan. It started with a problem we didn't understand yet.

## Unknown territory means you discuss before you build

When the path is clear, discussion is overhead. When it isn't, discussion _is_ the
work. This team defaulted to talking things through — not endless meetings, but
tight, high-bandwidth conversations where engineering, UX, and delivery hashed out
the actual problem together.

The temptation in ambiguity is to reduce anxiety by building _something_ fast. That
feels like progress. It usually isn't. We resisted it and spent the early time
making the problem smaller instead of the solution bigger.

## Collaborating with UX as engineers, not clients

The failure mode is treating UX as a vendor: they hand you mocks, you build them,
everyone's unhappy. What worked was sitting in the problem together:

- Engineers flagged what was cheap vs expensive _while_ designs were forming, so
  UX could trade freely.
- UX pushed back when the "easy" engineering choice quietly worsened the
  experience.
- We prototyped the risky interactions early instead of arguing about them in the
  abstract.

The willingness-to-use jump from 16% to 86% didn't come from a clever
implementation. It came from designing and building the risky parts _at the same
time_, so neither side committed to something the other couldn't support.

## Delivery in the room, not at the end

Delivery is usually where ambiguity goes to die — scope creep, missed dates,
finger-pointing. Having delivery in the discussions from the start meant scope was
negotiated continuously, not discovered at the deadline. When something got hard,
we cut the right thing early instead of the wrong thing late.

## The churn we saved was invisible

Here's the humbling part: the $1M we protected doesn't show up anywhere obvious.
There's no chart that goes up. Churn we prevented is a bad thing that _didn't
happen_. You only get credit for it if the team is honest about attribution and
looks for the counterfactual.

That's the real lesson of working in unknown territory:

> The highest-leverage work is often the least legible. If you want to do it,
> you need a team that can see value that isn't a line going up.

Talk out loud. Bring UX and delivery into the mess with you. And measure the bad
thing that didn't happen.
