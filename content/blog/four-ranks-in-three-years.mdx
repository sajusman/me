I joined Spekit as an Associate Engineer 1 and left three years later as a
Software Engineer 2 — four rungs up the ladder. People ask how. The honest answer
is that the thing that got me each promotion was different every time, and I had
to keep noticing when the old strategy stopped working.

## Level 1 → 2: Become reliable

Early on, promotion was almost entirely about **trust in my output**. Could
someone hand me a ticket and stop thinking about it? That's it.

The unlock wasn't working more hours. It was reducing the number of times my work
came back. I started:

- Reading the ticket _and_ the surrounding code before writing anything.
- Testing the unhappy paths, not just the demo path.
- Leaving the code cleaner than I found it, so reviewers trusted my PRs.

Boring. Effective.

## Level 2 → 3: Own outcomes, not tasks

The jump that surprised me was that "being a great task-doer" stopped mattering.
The next level rewarded owning an _outcome_ — something with a fuzzy edge that
nobody had fully specced.

For me that was the Design System. Nobody assigned me "build a design system with
ChakraUI, document it in Storybook, and get every squad to adopt it." I saw that
inconsistent UI was slowing everyone down, proposed it, and drove it. Delivery
velocity went up ~40% across squads.

> The moment you stop asking "what should I build?" and start asking "what's worth
> building?", you've changed levels — the title just catches up later.

## Level 3 → 4: Multiply other people

The last jump was the least about code. The revamp that drove $1M+ in ARR — where
willingness-to-use went from 16% to 86% — wasn't a solo effort. My job shifted
from _writing the code_ to _making the code get written well_:

- Mentoring new joiners so they ramped in weeks, not months.
- Spreading product knowledge so decisions didn't bottleneck on me.
- Refactoring the legacy codebase so the whole team moved 80% faster.

At this level, my leverage was other engineers. A promotion is really a company
saying "we've noticed you make the people around you better."

## The meta-lesson

Each level, I had to _fire my old self_. The habits that made me great at level 2
were exactly the ceiling at level 3. Growth wasn't doing more of the same thing —
it was noticing when the same thing had stopped being the point.

If you're stuck, the question usually isn't "how do I work harder?" It's "what is
this level actually rewarding, and am I still optimizing for the last one?"
