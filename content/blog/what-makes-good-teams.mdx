I've worked remote from Sydney, remote across US timezones, and onsite in Berlin.
Different products, different stacks, different funding stages. The teams that
consistently shipped good work weren't the ones with the most talent in the
room — they were the ones with a few boring habits done relentlessly.

## Good teams make decisions cheap to reverse

The best teams I've been on optimized for _reversibility_, not correctness. Nobody
waited three weeks for the perfect architecture. We shipped the smallest thing
that could teach us something, put it behind a flag, and watched it.

> The goal isn't to be right on the first try. The goal is to be wrong quickly
> and cheaply.

At Spekit, this is how we shipped embeddings-based search into the extension. We
didn't design the "final" retrieval system. We shipped a naive vector match,
measured it against a survey, and iterated. The 20% effectiveness bump came from
_iterations_, not from the first design.

## They over-communicate the "why", not the "what"

Weak teams hand you tickets. Strong teams hand you problems.

When I built Faircado's visual search, nobody handed me a spec for image cropping
and object detection. I got the outcome we wanted — _let users search by pointing
at a thing_ — and the freedom to figure out the how. That trust is what let me
choose the model, own the cropping UX, and ship something that lifted retention
25%.

## They treat UX and delivery as teammates, not stakeholders

The churn-saving work I'm proudest of only happened because engineering, UX, and
delivery were in the same room arguing about the same problem. Not a handoff. Not
a "requirements doc". A shared whiteboard and a shared incentive.

## They keep the codebase honest

- Migrate the legacy thing before it calcifies.
- Convert JS to TS while you're in there anyway.
- Write the test that would have caught the bug you just fixed.

None of this is heroic. It's hygiene. But teams that skip it spend their velocity
on firefighting, and teams that don't get to spend it on the product.

## The short version

A good team is a group of people who trust each other enough to move fast, and
respect the codebase enough to keep it possible. Everything else is detail.
